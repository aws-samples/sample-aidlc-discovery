The shape of the day

Five phases, one relay of files, and three rules that hold it together.

Your winDraw the whole method from memory: each phase, the question it asks, and the file it hands to the next. That is the first slide of any presentation, and the map a new facilitator needs before any detail.

Why the method exists

Teams tend to build what the loudest voice asks for. Discovery puts the team's beliefs on record, tests them against customer evidence, and ends with a decision: commit, pivot, or park.The framing used to open train-the-trainer.md, "Why and how". It takes about 6.5 working hours, and the team leaves with one document the build starts from: the Discovery brief.

The core idea is old. Amazon's Working Backwards starts from the press release, not the technology, so the team builds only what it can explain to a customer.Werner Vogels, "Working Backwards", 2006. What's new is that an assistant does the drafting and the analysis, so the room can spend its time deciding.

Five questions, asked in order

Each phase answers one question. The order matters: you can't pick a problem before you've heard customers, and you can't test a solution before you've named the assumption that would kill it.

PhaseThe questionWhat it does
SignalsWhat are customers telling us?Records the team's beliefs, then ranks the customer problems in the data, with counts and quotes.
Working BackwardsWhich problem first, for whom, and how would we announce it?Personas, scoring that picks one problem, then the press release, PRD, and riskiest assumption.
PrototypeWhat would it look like?A clickable prototype aimed at the riskiest assumption, critiqued by the personas, changed once.
ValidateWhat could make it fail, and how will we know it worked?Four risks, a pre-mortem, success criteria, and the decision.
HandoffWhat does the build start from?The Discovery brief and the next steps.

Setup comes first (product, people, business metric, data). The readout and the 7-, 30-, and 60-day follow-ups come after.The questions are verbatim from the phase table in README.md.

The relay: each phase hands over a file

The phases don't share memory; they share files. A phase can't start until the file it needs exists. This is what lets a team stop and pick up next week, and what lets you jump into the middle of a run when one stalls.The entry skill's To start, it needs table, in aidlc-discovery/SKILL.md.

Look at what crosses each arrow. Prototype needs the riskiest assumption, not just the PRD, because a prototype that tests nothing is a demo. Validate needs the persona critique, because it is the main evidence for whether the idea is valuable and usable. Handoff needs the decision, because the brief is written for every outcome, including park.

Three rules hold it together

  1. The assistant drafts, the team decides. Every phase ends at a checkpoint. No score or decision is final until the team confirms it. The facilitator's job is the room: time, energy, and making sure the team does the thinking.
  2. Every claim carries a label. Known (the data shows it), Assumed (believed, not shown), or Needs evidence (it matters, and has to be tested). The labels are how unproven ideas survive the day as honest open questions instead of hidden ones.
  3. No architecture. Discovery decides who, what, and why. How to build it belongs to the build.

The skill has seven shared rules in all; these three are the ones a room notices.Full list: phase map, "The rules that run through every stage".

Whatever you missed is what to say out loud first next time you present.

1. What does the team do before Signals shows any analysis?
Beliefs go on record first, because AI analysis is persuasive. With the expectations written down, the report can show where the data agrees and where it doesn't. Quotes come at the end of Signals; weights and the riskiest assumption come later.
2. Which files must exist before Prototype can start?
The PRD says what to show; the riskiest assumption says what the prototype must test. The persona critique is something Prototype produces, and Validate needs.
3. A claim in the PRD is believed by the team but not shown in the data. Which label does it carry?
Assumed. "Open" is a verdict on a risk in Validate, and "Draft" is an artifact status on the progress board. The riskiest assumption is chosen from the Assumed claims.
4. Which three outcomes can Validate end with?
Commit, pivot, or park. The others are real, which is why they're easy to mix up: build first, defer, and drop are scoring decisions; green, amber, and red are the readiness verdict on the PR/FAQ; supported, contradicted, and open are the verdicts on each of the four risks.
5. Who makes the build decision?
The team. The assistant drafts and makes the evidence visible; the facilitator runs the room. The sponsor is asked only if the team can't agree, at the readout.
6. What never goes into the Discovery brief?
No architecture: Discovery decides who, what, and why. Open hypotheses are section 14, prototype screens section 11, success criteria section 7.
