Lesson 0005 · About 15 minutes

Prototype

Something to react to, aimed at the one assumption that would end the idea, critiqued by the personas, and changed once.

Your winRun the prototype hour so it tests something: check the screen plan for a test moment, let the persona critique do the work after a "looks good", steer the iteration away from polish, and log every unconfirmed promise as Needs evidence.

What this phase is for

A prototype gives the team and its personas something to react to, and that teaches more than another document. It isn't a release: it is one self-contained HTML file with no backend, built so the riskiest assumption can be tested in it.discovery-prototype/SKILL.md, Overview. It needs prd.md and riskiest-assumption.md, and runs 13:45 to 14:45.

The common failure says it all: the prototype looks finished but tests nothing. Every step below guards against it.

Five steps

  1. Plan the screens. 4 to 8: the entry point, the main task's steps, the confirmation, and the main unhappy path. One line per screen: what the persona sees and does, and which P0 requirement it carries. One screen is marked the test moment, where the riskiest assumption becomes visible. The team confirms, cuts, or adds. A focused flow beats a complete app.
  2. Build from the shell. Every version is an exact copy of the shell. Only the region between the EDIT BELOW and EDIT ABOVE markers changes: settings and one template per screen. The shell provides the Flow board, the phone and browser frames, the routes, and the "Show test moment" toggle. It opens on the Flow view, so the team sees the whole journey first.
  3. Team click-through, in Mobile or Desktop, whichever the main persona uses. "Where would this person hesitate? Does the test moment make the assumption visible?" Note reactions; change nothing yet.
  4. Persona critique. Each persona walks the core flow in its own voice: where it gets stuck, which of its pains are solved, how it fits its workflow, what would make it switch, whether the riskiest assumption holds, and a day-one adoption score from 1 to 10. The satisfied persona also says what it would lose.persona-critique.md
  5. Iterate. The team chooses which findings to fix. Every iteration is a new file (v2.html, v3.html…), never an overwrite, logged in versions.md with the finding behind each change. index.html is a copy of the current version; compare.html shows versions side by side.

The quality bar includes: every P0 on a screen, every screen reachable, the test moment within two taps of the start, realistic content with no placeholder text, one accent colour, and accessible contrast and tap targets.html-prototype.md, Quality bar.

Air: what the personas found

The Air plan has seven screens. The test moment is "Ticket issued", the confirmation the passenger must trust, two taps from the disruption message.prototype/notes.md Here is Tomasz, the weekly business traveller, on v1:

Promises are questions for the build

The agency notice shows the rule. A fix that promises something nobody has confirmed (holding seats, sending health or financial data by link, a real-time feed) isn't a design detail. Before adding it, tell the team what it depends on and who must confirm it. If they keep it, log it as Needs evidence in versions.md and list it in notes.md as a question for Validate.

Running the room

  • Changes to the plan: when the assistant changes what the team agreed (a path dropped, a shortcut added), read each change and ask its owner "Keep it, or change it back?" A change nobody decided becomes a surprise at the readout.
  • Claims on screen that nobody has confirmed (a bag that moves, a booking that is final) are marked Needs evidence. Ask the person who would know.
  • Factual questions: when the assistant asks one (what time does the call-back line close?), get the answer from the person who knows, or mark it Needs evidence. A guessed fact in the prototype becomes a promise in the brief. In the Air run with a team, "call back at any hour" became "when the line opens at 07:00" for exactly this reason.
  • Choosing fixes: fix first what touches the riskiest assumption, then what is cheap; send the rest to Validate with an owner. If a finding changes what the assumption means, update it now. In the run with a team, the critique found passengers trusted the new flight but would still call about a hotel, so "won't call to check" widened to "won't need to call for anything".anycompany-air-with-team/…/versions.md; riskiest-assumption.md.
  • In a team Space, every version is saved to the Space and opens in the session tab from there, so anyone can click through it in their own Amazon Quick. The compare page shows empty frames in Amazon Quick: open compare.html from the working folder in a browser.
  • "Looks good" at the first click-through is normal. The critique finds the problems; let the room pick which to fix.
  • Iterating on colours? Ask what the persona would trip over instead. Stop when it's good enough to start a conversation, not when it's finished.

When it goes wrong: a plain single page means it didn't build from the shell. Say: "Load modules/discovery-prototype/references/prototype-shell.md and copy that HTML exactly, filling only the edit region", and ask it to quote the EDIT BELOW line first.run-sheet.md, 13:45 and "When things go wrong".

Checkpoint: "Here is what your personas said and what we changed. Ready to try to break it?"

Practice

Recall first: the six things each persona answers in the critique, plus the satisfied persona's extra question. Then open this.

Walk-through (where I'd hesitate or give up) · my pain (solved and not) · my workflow · switching (what would make me drop my workaround, what would stop me) · the riskiest assumption (does it hold?) · day-one adoption, 1 to 10. The satisfied persona adds: what does this change that already works for me, and what would I lose?

1. What must the screen plan mark before anything is built?
The test moment is where the riskiest assumption becomes visible, and it must be reachable within two taps. There is no backend, and P1 features are left out on purpose.
2. At the first click-through the team says only "looks good". What do you do?
Teams often find little on their own first pass; that's why the critique runs before the iteration. Polishing before the critique is a listed "don't".
3. A critique fix adds "We've sent this to your travel agency", which nobody has confirmed the systems can do. What happens?
Tell the team what it depends on and who must confirm it. If it stays, log it as Needs evidence in versions.md and as a question for Validate in notes.md. It reappears in the brief's open hypotheses.
4. Tomasz says "Ticket issued" isn't proof and he'd call anyway. Which risk does that point to?
Valuable: it's the riskiest assumption, that passengers trust the confirmation enough not to call. He can use the flow fine; the question is whether it changes what he does.
5. For the iteration, the team wants a new accent colour and different fonts. Your move?
Colour iteration is a listed common failure. Bring the room back to the critique's findings. If a theme change really is needed, it goes through the theme setting, never by editing the shell.
6. Why is an earlier version never overwritten?
The team may want to go back, and Validate chooses the version to hand off, with compare.html showing them side by side. The brief describes the chosen version and one line on how versions evolved.
7. From lesson 0004: which readiness question is most often the gap?
The full future journey, then the slice the prototype will show. It's the readiness answer this phase builds from, which is why a vague one makes a prototype that tests nothing.
8. The prototype arrives as one plain page, with no Flow, Mobile, or Desktop views. What's the fix?
It didn't build from the shell. Have it load prototype-shell.md, quote the EDIT BELOW line to prove it read it, and copy the shell exactly. Patching the views in by hand rebuilds what the shell already gives you.
