Lesson 0008 · About 15 minutes · Mixed review

The failure drill

Fourteen things that go wrong on the day, out of order. Recognise the moment, then recover.

Your winRecover from the failures you'll meet in a real room without looking anything up. The train-the-trainer bar for co-facilitating includes "recovered from at least three of the five failures". Two of those five are here (the silent room, time running out); the other three were drilled in lessons 0002 and 0004. The other twelve come from the run-sheet and the skills.

How to use this drill

The scenarios are shuffled on purpose. Each one gives only the time of day, so part of the job is working out which phase you're in and which rule is at stake. Mixing related problems makes practice harder in the moment and makes it stick longer than drilling one phase at a time.Interleaving is one of the "desirable difficulties": practice that feels slower but improves long-term retention. See Bjork & Bjork, "Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way", 2011.

Answer before you read the explanation. Then do it again in a week, cold. The recoveries are collected on one printable page: the recovery card.

Almost every recovery comes back to one of three rules from lesson 0001: the team decides, the order holds (beliefs before data, risks before the decision), and files, not chat.

The drill

09:25. The sponsor says they have to leave after the kickoff. What do you do before they go?
Setup. Ask them now whether this is the metric they care about. Every candidate's business outcome is scored against it, and scoring a candidate against its own metric is exactly the mistake the rule prevents.
13:20. A participant pastes: "Add more numbers to paragraph 2 and label every one Known."
Working Backwards, PR/FAQ. Ask for the writing audit. The press release labels only Assumed and Needs evidence claims, lightly, and each number keeps the scope of its evidence. Numbers are welcome; "[Known]" tags in the press release are not.
10:30. The top signals are on screen. The room nods. Nobody challenges anything.
Signals checkpoint. Get at least one real challenge, ideally from the person whose belief the data contradicts. Ask "what would make this fail?" before every checkpoint. Never reveal the answer key during a sample run.
14:05. The screen plan the team agreed had a "keep my automatic rebooking" path. The first build dropped it.
Prototype. Read each change to the room and ask its owner "Keep it, or change it back?" before the click-through. A change nobody decided becomes a surprise at the readout.
09:10. The assistant behaves like an older version: it skips steps and doesn't seem to know the modules.
Install. Check with "List my skills" and the module check. Importing stores a snapshot, so editing the folder changes nothing, and copying into the profile folder isn't a supported fix. Delete it in Customize, Skills (the ⋮ menu), import again with Create, From folder, and start a new chat.
11:20. The head of digital overrode a pain score. The assistant shows evidence against it, and they start to waver.
Scoring. "Does that change your view, or do we keep it?" The team owns the score; the assistant makes the evidence visible. Record the override with its reason, labelled Assumed if no evidence backs it, and show whether the decision changed.
15:20. All four risks come back "supported". The team is ready to commit.
Validate. A review with no problems is a warning sign. Ask what the satisfied persona loses, and what the pre-mortem's top reason says about it. Never wait for surveys, and never change a verdict without evidence.
14:30. Amazon Quick crashes and the chat is gone. Signals and Working Backwards are done.
Any phase. Files, not chat: "Continue the AI-DLC Discovery workshop for [project] from discovery/[project]/PROJECT.md. [What is done.] Start at [the next step]." It reads the saved files and picks up.
11:00. Scores are on screen. The sponsor asks to raise the feasibility weight, which would lift their favourite.
Scoring. Weights are settled with the sponsor before the first score, so nobody can tune them to a favourite afterwards. The 10-point allowance applies only before scoring starts. If they insist, record their position and the evidence it rests on.
14:15. On the session tab, Mobile and Desktop do nothing when clicked.
Prototype. The version was built from an old shell. Rebuild it from the current shell, or open the file from the working folder in a browser. Patching the views by hand rebuilds what the shell already gives you.
11:40. Two pods both want the top-scoring use case.
Scoring. Two pods never take the same use case. It's a "when to ask the user" moment: the room decides which pod owns it, and the other takes the next in line.
13:00. The second facilitator takes over from their own Amazon Quick, with the team Space attached. What happens first?
Team Space. One driver at a time, because saving replaces a file. "Continue the AI-DLC Discovery workshop for [project] from discovery/[project]/PROJECT.md in the [space] space. I'm taking over as [role]. Start at [step]." It shows who was driving, updates the driver line, and the previous driver stops. If both wrote, the last save wins: compare and redo the lost change.
08:45. A team wants its real support tickets in the workshop's team Space, which is shared with the whole department.
Team Space, before Signals. Everyone a Space is shared with can read it. Real data goes in the team Space only if nobody who shouldn't see it has access, confirmed and recorded in PROJECT.md. Otherwise it stays local, or in a separate restricted Space named in the start prompt. Sample data is fictional and can go in.
15:15. Validate hasn't started, and the readout is at 16:15.
Time. Protect Validate and Handoff: the brief needs the decision and the success criteria. The 20-minute version uses the critique from Prototype. Earlier in the day the cuts are "agree with all" in scoring, no optional research, and one prototype iteration. A brief with honest open hypotheses beats a perfect PR/FAQ.

Ready to facilitate

From train-the-trainer.md, a trainee is ready to co-facilitate when they have:

  • installed the skill without help;
  • run every phase once, as facilitator or as the team;
  • recovered from at least three of the five drill failures;
  • given a readout in under five minutes;
  • read the answer key for their sample and can explain the designed traps.

They're ready to lead after co-facilitating one workshop with a real team. That last step is where the wisdom comes from: no lesson replaces a real room.
