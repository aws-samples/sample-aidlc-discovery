Lesson 0006 · About 15 minutes

Validate

Spend 45 minutes trying to prove it fails, with only what's in the room. Then decide.

Your winRun Validate so the decision rests on evidence: give each of the four risks a verdict, run a pre-mortem the room owns, write success and kill criteria with numbers and dates, and turn "commit" into conditions when the riskiest assumption is still open.

What this phase is for

Validate answers: what could make this fail, and how will we know it worked? It uses only the data, the personas, and the prototype. It never waits for surveys. Anything it can't settle stays an open hypothesis and goes into the brief; real users test the prototype in the follow-up.discovery-validate/SKILL.md, Overview. It needs prototype/index.html and persona-critique.md, and runs 15:00 to 15:45.

The order matters. When the team wants to decide before the review, do the four risks first: the decision rests on them.

1. Re-walk what changed

The critique judged v1; Validate judges the version the team will hand off. For five minutes, the primary personas walk v2, and only for the findings the iteration changed. In Air, Tomasz: "The e-ticket number and 'Flight 2417 removed from your trip' are what I check when I call. If they're here, I don't call." Finding fixed, and a new doubt: "'Sent to your travel agency' had better be true."Air risk-review.md, Re-walk.

2. Four risks

The four risks come from Marty Cagan: value, usability, feasibility, business viability.Marty Cagan, "The Four Big Risks", SVPG. Each gets an assumption, evidence for and against, a verdict (supported, contradicted, or open), and the test that would settle it.

RiskThe questionMain evidenceAir verdict
ValuableWill they want it enough to change what they do?Critique (pain, switching), signalsSupported
UsableCan each persona finish the core flow unaided?Critique walk-through, re-walkSupported; open for refunds
FeasibleIs there a known path to build it?The engineering lead; PRD dependenciesOpen
ViableDoes it work for the business?The GTM viability checkOpen, nothing blocking

Feasibility names unknowns, not an architecture. Air's is open because today's agent-led changes already fail (tickets not reissued, the app still showing the cancelled flight). The test that settles it: the engineering lead walks 50 recent rebookings and lists where they failed.

Then the riskiest assumption is checked explicitly. Air: open, leaning supported. v1 contradicted it for two personas, v2 fixed that, but the personas are synthetic and real passengers have been burned before.

If every risk comes back supported, ask what the satisfied persona loses, and what the pre-mortem's top reason says.

3. Pre-mortem

"It's 90 days after launch and the feature failed. Why?" Imagining the failure has already happened makes it easier for people to say what worries them.Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem", HBR, 2007. Everyone writes a reason before anyone speaks, then go round, starting with the most optimistic person. Only then does the assistant add its own from the evidence. Keep the five most likely, each with an early warning sign and a prevention step. Air's top one:

"'Ticket issued' was sometimes wrong, and passengers learned to call anyway." Warning sign: calls within 24 hours from passengers who self-rebooked. Prevention: the feasibility check before the build, and zero mismatches in 50 internal changes.

4. Success criteria, then the GTM completed

3 to 5 criteria, each with a metric, baseline, target, and check date (30, 60, 90 days), consistent with the North Star. Air's first: self-service rebooking rate, baseline 12%, target 20% at 30 days, 30% at 60, 35% at 90. Vanity metrics fail; tie each one to the business metric or to adoption. With no baseline, record who gets it by the 7-day follow-up.

Then discovery-gtm completes its required parts:discovery-gtm, mode complete; Air gtm.md.

  • Viability check: legal and compliance, privacy and security, sales, support and success, marketing, each with an owner role and a concern or "none". All "none" means ask again, area by area.
  • Adoption and success: an adoption owner on the product engineering team (not marketing), leading indicators, targets, and kill criteria: a specific number by a specific date. Air: "below 18% on release routes 60 days into the limited release, or more than 25% of self-rebookers calling within 24 hours: stop the rollout."

Every GTM part is then marked validated or open; nothing stays "draft". If the review changed a score, discovery-use-case-scoring re-scores, with each change confirmed and recorded as "after Validate".

5. The decision

First, pick the prototype version to hand off. Then summarise in three lines (supported, contradicted, open) and ask: commit, pivot, or park, and why?

Commit
Build it. If the riskiest assumption is still open, ask one more question: commit to building now, or to testing first? Testing first means conditions that must be true before the build starts, each with four things: what is measured, the threshold, who checks it, and by which follow-up. Check them against what the team agreed in Working Backwards, so the two don't contradict each other.
Pivot
Keep what was learned and reframe. The reframed or next-ranked use case is taken up at 30 days.
Park
Stop for now and keep everything: the evidence, and what would reopen it. A validated decision not to build is a good outcome.

Air: commit, testing the riskiest assumption and feasibility first. Three conditions, each with an owner, due by day 30: 6 of 8 recently disrupted passengers complete v2 and say they wouldn't call; the engineering lead confirms a reissue path; a sample of 100 calls shows at least 40% are simple cases.Air decision.md

If the room gives its decision before the review is done, the assistant saves it as the team's intent, with what is still open, and records the decision only after the review. That is correct. The decision is never made for the team. If it can't agree, record each position and its evidence, and ask the sponsor at the readout. Short on time? The 20-minute version is the four risks and the decision, using the critique from Prototype.

Practice

Recall first: the five parts of Validate in order, and the three decisions. Then open this.

Re-walk → four risks (with the riskiest assumption) → pre-mortem → success criteria (then GTM completed, and re-scoring if needed) → decision. Commit (with conditions if testing first), pivot, or park.

Also saved: prioritization.md, which ranks the customer problems by frequency, severity, and reach with the open hypotheses. It's the backlog the brief carries, separate from use case scoring.

1. Once its inputs are loaded, what does Validate do first?
The critique judged the first build; the re-walk checks that the iteration fixed what it found, in five minutes. Validate never waits for surveys, and re-scoring happens only if the review changes something.
2. The engineering lead knows some path exists but can't say whether a self-service change will issue the ticket every time. Feasibility verdict?
Open, with the test that would settle it, an owner, and a date. With no evidence either way, the verdict is open. "Dropped" is a scoring decision, not a risk verdict.
3. Which of these is a usable success criterion?
It has a metric, a baseline, a target, and a check date, and it is the business metric agreed at setup. Downloads are a vanity metric; the other two can't be measured as written.
4. In the pre-mortem, whose reasons come first?
The room's first. The reasons the team already worries about are the ones most worth writing down, and they're anchored away if the assistant goes first.
5. The team says "commit", but the riskiest assumption is still open. What do you ask?
If it's testing first, record the conditions that must be true before the build, who checks each, and by which follow-up. The brief carries them in its Decision section. Air chose to test first.
6. Which is a well-formed kill criterion?
A specific number by a specific date. Kill criteria that never trigger are a listed common failure.
7. The team decides to park. What is park?
Stop for now, keep the evidence, and record what would reopen it. The brief is still written: a parked idea's evidence is worth keeping.
8. From lesson 0005: where must the prototype's test moment be?
It's on the quality bar. In Air, "Ticket issued" is two taps from the disruption message, which is why the re-walk can reach it in minutes.
